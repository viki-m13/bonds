Can Anyone Beat Just Buying QQQ?

A plain-English research review of stock-picking, market timing, and why a boring automatic index purchase keeps winning. Every statistic below was computed from point-in-time market data — the record as it actually looked on each historical day, dead companies included.
The verdict: on 26 years of honest data, no method of picking stocks or timing markets reliably ends up with more money than automatically buying QQQ every two weeks or every month. Not expert selection, not "buying the winners," not value screens, not dip-buying, not machine learning. This isn't because markets are magic. It's arithmetic: a few huge winners produce nearly all stock-market wealth, the index already owns them at full size and rides them automatically, and every act of picking makes you own less of them. The rest of this page shows the evidence, then answers every objection we could find.

1 · The rules of the test

The question: investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule (every two weeks or monthly — both tested, the difference is negligible), can any rule for choosing stocks or timing purchases end with more money than putting every contribution into QQQ?

Three rules make the test honest, and they matter more than any strategy:

  • Point-in-time data. Every simulated decision uses only information that existed on that day. No hindsight.
  • The dead are counted. The data includes every stock that later went bankrupt, was delisted, or was acquired — at its real final price. Most "market-beating" backtests you'll see online quietly use only companies that still exist today, which is like studying lottery winners to conclude lotteries pay well. (Of the 2,177 investable stocks in 2016, 666 no longer traded by 2026.)
  • Same money, same dates. The benchmark receives identical contributions on identical days, and strategies pay realistic trading costs.

QQQ holds the 100 largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq, weighted by size — a mechanical rule, refreshed automatically, expense ratio 0.20%. SPY holds the S&P 500. 2.1  Why a top-100 index keeps beating the broader 500

Mechanically, QQQ is a purer expression of the one force this whole paper documents: letting winners run at full weight. Fewer names means the mega-winners are a larger share; size-weighting means their weight grows automatically as they win. No judgment is involved — just less dilution of the lottery tickets that §3 shows produce everything.

2.2  “But choosing QQQ as the benchmark is itself hindsight — isn't this whole study circular?”

This is the sharpest objection to this paper, so it gets a full logical answer. Start by separating two claims that are usually blurred together:

Claim A (benchmark-independent): no method of selecting stocks or timing purchases reliably beats an automatic, cap-weighted index — whichever index you choose.

Claim B (benchmark-specific): QQQ has been the better index to automate this era, and choosing it going forward is a deliberate, concentrated bet.

The study's verdict rests entirely on Claim A. Claim B only decides which machine you automate — and §9 states its risk openly.

Why Claim A cannot be an artifact of picking a hot benchmark — four steps:

  • 1. Swap the benchmark; the verdict survives. If "nothing beats QQQ" were true only because QQQ ran hot, pickers would beat a cooler benchmark. They don't: the same 100 random 10-stock portfolios that ended at a median 0.58× of QQQ-DCA also lost to SPY-DCA — median 0.84×, with two-thirds trailing the broader index too. And the professional scorecard (SPIVA) doesn't use QQQ at all: it measures every fund against its own category benchmark — value funds vs value indexes, small-cap funds vs small-cap indexes — and finds ~90% failure everywhere over 15 years [12]. A conclusion that survives substituting the benchmark is, by definition, not benchmark selection bias; the choice of QQQ changes the size of the picker's shortfall, not its existence.
  • 2. The core mechanism is an identity, not a sample. For any index over any universe, the average actively-managed dollar in that universe must equal the index before costs and trail it after costs (Sharpe's arithmetic [7]) — true in 1929, true in 2000, true if technology collapses tomorrow. And the skew that makes concentrated picking worse than average is measured across a century of U.S. data [1] and 42 countries [31]. Neither premise mentions QQQ.
  • 3. Even perfect benchmark hindsight doesn't rescue picking. Grant the picker our exact "bias": tell them in 2015 that U.S. mega-cap tech will win. Picking inside the winning pond still lost — the era's dominant giants beat QQQ only 11/40 times across four decade-cohorts; momentum strategies restricted to the index's own members died in audit; the tech-theme funds trailed (§11.5). The benchmark's era explains the benchmark's return; it does not create the picker's shortfall. The shortfall comes from skew + costs, which follow the picker into any pond.
  • 4. The rule predates the result. QQQ is not a basket assembled after seeing what won — the Nasdaq-100 rule dates to 1985 and the ETF to 1999, before every window measured here. Contrast the actual hindsight construction people confuse it with: "the Magnificent 7" is a list defined by past winning, and §10b shows what buying such lists ex-ante actually did. This study never benchmarks against anything defined by the outcome.

2.3  The asymmetry that settles it: one bounded choice vs a repeated unbounded game

Finally, the logical difference between choosing an index and choosing stocks — the reason the first decision is safe to make imperfectly and the second isn't:

Worst realistic outcome of each decision, as measured in this study (relative to the alternative, horizons as labeled).

QQQ and SPY are two overlapping winner-riding machines: SPY also held Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia — at somewhat smaller weights. Choose the "wrong" one and you still capture the winning tail automatically; the maximum regret over 26 years was a factor of ~2, while still multiplying your money ~8×. It is one decision between two self-correcting rules, with bounded regret. Stock selection is the opposite object: a repeated game (hundreds of decisions, each with measured sub-50% odds, costs compounding) with unbounded regret — the median pick captures a quarter of the index's decade and the tail outcome is zero. Choosing between trains headed the same direction is not the same kind of decision as betting you can outrun them. That is why this paper agonizes over stock-picking and treats the QQQ-vs-SPY choice as a one-paragraph preference.

3 · The market is a lottery with a few winning tickets — and the index holds them all

Here is every investable U.S. stock at mid-2016 — all 2,177 of them, including the 666 that later died — and what each returned over the following decade:

6.2%
That's the fraction of stocks that beat QQQ over that decade — roughly 1 in 16. A quarter lost money outright in one of the best decades markets have ever had. The median stock returned +71% in total while QQQ returned +638%.

And the gains that do exist are brutally concentrated: the top 1% of stocks produced ~19% of all net gains; the top 10% produced ~61%. This is the same picture the canonical academic result paints (Bessembinder [1]) — a small sliver of companies accounts for most of the stock market's entire net wealth creation since 1926, and most individual stocks underperform cash.

The published record says the same thing

Bessembinder (2018), studying every U.S. stock since 1926 (~26,000 companies): the best-performing 4% of companies account for the stock market's entire net gain over Treasury bills; the other 96% collectively matched cash. Four out of seven stocks returned less than Treasury bills over their lifetime, and just five companies produced 10% of all wealth ever created [1]. His global follow-up found it's even more extreme worldwide.

J.P. Morgan's “Agony & Ecstasy” study (Russell 3000, 1980–2014): 40% of all stocks suffered a catastrophic decline of 70%+ from which they never recovered; two-thirds underperformed the index; the median stock lagged it by −54% over its lifetime [2].

Heaton, Polson & Witte, “Why Indexing Works” (2017) formalized the mechanism this section shows empirically: when a few stocks drive everything, any subset you pick most likely misses them, so most pickers must trail the index — before costs [3].

Cumulative share of ALL net gains (2016–2026) produced by the top N stocks, log scale. Ten stocks — 0.4% of the market — produced 10.7% of everything; 250 stocks produced 61%.

It's not one lucky decade. Year by year since 2000, the fraction of stocks beating QQQ over the following 12 months averaged 42% — and the years it exceeded 50% are mostly 2000–2001, when "beating QQQ" meant falling less than an index that was crashing:

% of stocks beating QQQ over the next 12 months, each year 2000–2025 (dashed line = coin flip)

This is the whole game. Picking stocks means trading away guaranteed full-size ownership of the few tickets that pay for a handful of tickets that each probably won't. The index isn't smart — it just refuses to hand any winning ticket back.

4 · Why “buying the winners” specifically fails

The most seductive idea in investing: just buy what's already winning. Three measured facts kill it.

4.1 The winners list doesn't stay the winners list

15%
Of each year's top-10% performers, that's how many repeat in the top 10% the following year. 56% fall below the median stock; only 38% beat QQQ. (Measured across 24 annual cohorts, 2001–2024.)
The published record

The same non-persistence holds for professionals: S&P's Persistence Scorecard finds that of funds in the top quartile in any year, almost none remain top-quartile a few years later — at rates at or below what chance predicts [4]. Momentum itself is a real, Nobel-adjacent finding (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993 [5]) — but it's a short-horizon relative effect that decays and periodically crashes (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016 [6]); it is not "good stocks keep being good."

4.2 Buying the hot list and holding is a disaster

Done literally on point-in-time data: at the start of 2015, buy the 20 hottest stocks in America (the top of the 12-month leaderboard), $1,000 each, and hold:

Why so bad? Because the top of a 12-month leaderboard is where fragile, over-extended, often speculative names congregate — the measured odds of an extreme recent winner beating QQQ over the next year are just 5–17%, the worst forward odds of any group studied.

The index doesn't just find the winners — it forces you to keep them.

The published record

Sharpe's "Arithmetic of Active Management" (1991) is the iron law underneath all of this: the average actively-managed dollar must earn the market return before costs, and less than it after costs — always, by accounting identity. Beating the index is a zero-sum game played against professionals, minus fees [7].

Fama & French, "Luck versus Skill" (2010): across 3,000+ mutual funds, so few beat their benchmarks net of costs that the winners are statistically indistinguishable from what luck alone would produce [8]. Carhart (1997) found the same decades earlier: no persistent fund skill beyond fees and momentum exposure [9].

McLean & Pontiff (2016): even published academic anomalies lose ~26% of their returns after their sample period ends and ~58% after publication — edges die on contact with daylight [10]. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) showed most published factors likely aren't real at all once you correct for how many were tested — the same multiple-testing trap our audits caught [11].

6 · About your market-beating friend: the luck math

A concentrated stock portfolio beats QQQ in a given year roughly 40–45% of the time (that's the measured base rate — losing more often than winning, but not by much in any single year). Now imagine 10,000 people each running one portfolio, with zero skill, just those coin odds:

Expected number (out of 10,000 zero-skill pickers) still holding a perfect beat-the-market streak of each length

After five years, ~185 flawless five-year track records exist by pure chance. After ten years — three. Those people are not lying about their returns; they're real, they're confident, and they're indistinguishable from skilled until the streak ends. They post on YouTube; the 9,815 others don't. Survivorship bias isn't just a data problem — it's your entire social-media feed.

The published record

SPIVA (S&P, year-end 2024): 89.5% of professional U.S. large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years — full-time managers, with research teams, before your tax disadvantages [12]. Buffett's famous 10-year bet (2008–2017): a plain S&P 500 index fund returned +125.8%; the hand-picked hedge-fund portfolios averaged roughly +36% — he donated the winnings and repeated the advice: index [13].

And for the individual actually doing the trading: Barber & Odean (2000), 66,465 real brokerage households — the most active traders earned 11.4%/yr while the market returned 17.9% [14]. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap": real investors earn ~1.1 percentage points per year less than the very funds they hold, from timing their entries and exits [15]. A study of every Brazilian who began day-trading index futures (2013–2015): of those who persisted 300+ days, 97% lost money, and only 1.1% earned more than minimum wage [16].

6b · “So who actually DOES outperform — and how?”

Markets aren't unbeatable — they're unbeatable from where you're sitting. The honest census of who wins, and the mechanism each uses:

  • Market-makers and high-frequency firms — they don't predict anything; they sell liquidity, collecting a fraction of a cent on billions of trades (including yours). It's a toll booth, not a forecast, and it requires infrastructure you cannot rent.
  • A handful of closed quantitative funds (the famous one returns ~66%/yr gross) — thousands of tiny, fast statistical edges, capacity-capped at a few billion dollars, closed to outside money for decades. The same firm's funds that outsiders can buy have performed near the market. Capacity is the tell: real edges are small; anything sold to unlimited money isn't one.
  • Activists and private equity — they buy control and change the company. The return comes from doing, not picking.
  • Corporate insiders — the one real information edge, which is why trading on it is illegal (and why the legal shadow of it — cluster buying — was the best signal we measured).
  • Specialists in tiny, uncrowded niches — genuinely possible, capacity-limited, and a full-time job with a measured failure rate, not a strategy you buy.
  • The lucky — §6. By far the largest group, and indistinguishable from the skilled for about a decade (see below).

“Then how do hedge funds succeed?”

Mostly, the manager succeeds; the investor doesn't. The measured record:

Warren Buffett's public 10-year bet: S&P 500 index fund vs five hand-picked portfolios of hedge funds, 2008–2017 [13]
  • Corrected for reporting tricks, aggregate hedge-fund returns roughly match the index. Hedge-fund databases are self-reported: funds start reporting after a hot streak (backfill) and stop when they die (survivorship). Correcting both removes ~5–6 points of reported return per year [29]; corrected aggregate returns came out at 9.29%/yr vs the S&P's 9.38% over the classic study window [29].
  • The fee arithmetic is the business model: one analysis of the industry's whole history found investors collectively earned less than Treasury bills, while managers collected hundreds of billions in fees — the classic "2-and-20" is paid on assets, win or lose [30].
  • What institutions actually buy from the good funds isn't index-beating — it's returns uncorrelated with their stock portfolios (a diversification service). Judged against QQQ-DCA, that's a different product, not a refutation.
  • And the few genuinely great funds? See the census above: closed, capacity-capped, infrastructure businesses. The market-beating that exists is precisely the kind you cannot buy.

6c · The whole argument in probabilities

Strip away every story and the entire debate reduces to a few measured numbers. First, the one that explains every anecdote you've ever heard:

Three out of four picks made money — a rising market pays nearly everyone, which is why every picker you know has winners to tell you about. Beating the automatic machine is a different event:

The probability ladder, all point-in-time measurements from this study. Each step down is the same game, held longer.

Notice the shape: the odds fall as the horizon grows — time compounds the index's advantage, not the picker's. "In the long run it'll come back" is backwards: the long run is where picks go to lose.

And here is why the debate never dies: skill takes decades to even detect

Years of live results needed before performance is statistically distinguishable from luck (standard two-sigma test; IR = skill ratio). A "good" manager needs ~16 years — and the market a manager proved skill in no longer exists by the time it's proven.

This is the deepest reason the industry survives its own scorecard: at realistic skill levels, one investing lifetime is not long enough to tell a skilled picker from a lucky one — so belief fills the gap, marketing sells the belief, and the base rates quietly collect.

7 · The market-timing myths, measured

"Wait for the crash, then buy." Simulated directly: hold every contribution in cash until the index is at least 20% off its high, then invest it all. Since 2003:

invest every month → $3.25Mwait for −20% dips → $2.29M

Waiting for the obvious bargain cost ~30% of final wealth. Crashes are rare; cash waits for years earning nothing while the index compounds; and the "obvious" bottom is only obvious afterward. (In live crashes, the same people waiting for −20% decide at −20% to wait for −30%.)

"Get out when it looks dangerous, back in when it's safe." Every tested version of this — moving-average switches, volatility triggers, regime detectors — either lost outright or turned out to depend on which day of the month it happened to trade (the same rule returned anywhere from 0.74× to 3.31× depending on the trade date — a coin toss wearing a lab coat). None survived the audits.

8 · “Fine — then I'll just take more risk”

One thing does reliably end with more money in a rising market: more exposure. A 3×-leveraged version of the same index, same contributions:

contributionsSPYQQQ3× leveraged$1,000/month, 2006–2026, log scale

The red line ends highest — and fell −84% along the way; started three years earlier, it hits −99.9% in the dot-com crash and never recovers. That's the honest decoder for every impressive line that ends above QQQ in any backtest, anywhere: look for the extra risk. More risk is a legitimate choice; it is not skill, it doesn't need a guru, and almost no one actually holds through the part where it's down 84%.

9 · What this paper does NOT say

  • It does not say QQQ is safe. A QQQ investor in March 2000 fell -81% and waited roughly 15 years to break even on a lump sum. This is a concentrated technology bet, and this century has been technology's century — the next one may not be:
QQQ's distance below its own prior peak, 1999–2017 — the scar this paper refuses to hide
Growth of QQQ ÷ growth of SPY since 1999 (above 1× = QQQ ahead). Leadership rotates: QQQ spent roughly a decade below the line before its era — the next rotation is §9's whole point.
  • It does not say markets are perfectly efficient — only that the specific game of out-picking a winner-riding index using public information is stacked, measurably, against the picker.
  • It does not cover other asset classes. This is a study of stock strategies measured against QQQ; whether you hold anything besides stocks is a separate question it deliberately doesn't answer.
  • It does not promise the next 26 years look like the last. It says: whatever index you choose, no tested method of picking stocks against it has honestly beaten contributing to it on autopilot.

10b · Every objection we could find, answered

“But people do beat the market…”

“My friend / a guy on YouTube beats the market every year.”
Given how many people pick stocks, thousands of market-beaters must exist by pure chance. A single concentrated portfolio beats QQQ in a given year roughly 40–45% of the time (measured, §3). Start 10,000 people flipping that coin and after 5 years about 185 of them have a perfect streak — after 10 years, 3 still do — with zero skill involved (chart in §6). The lucky ones post; the other 9,800 don't. You only ever hear from the right tail.
“Warren Buffett beat the market for decades.”
He did — mostly in the 1950s–1990s, buying tiny neglected companies and whole private businesses, using cheap insurance-float leverage: a game a retail stock-picker cannot play. Over roughly the last two decades Berkshire has performed about in line with the S&P 500. Buffett himself won a famous 10-year, million-dollar bet that an index fund would beat a hand-picked group of hedge funds — and his standing advice for ordinary investors is a low-cost index fund.
“Renaissance's Medallion fund makes 60%+ a year.”
True, and instructive: Medallion is capped at roughly $10–15B (returns vanish at scale), closed to outside money for decades, and earns its returns from thousands of tiny, short-lived statistical edges executed with elite infrastructure — not from 'picking good stocks.' The same firm's funds that are open to outsiders have performed near the market. The existence of a closed, capacity-limited machine says nothing about what an individual can do in a brokerage account.
“Professionals with Bloomberg terminals and armies of analysts must beat it.”
The public scorecard says the opposite: S&P's SPIVA reports have shown, year after year, that 89.5% of professional U.S. large-cap funds lagged the S&P 500 over the 15 years through 2024 [12] — after fees, with every resource money can buy. The minority who lead in one period are not reliably the same ones who lead in the next (persistence studies). If the professionals can't, the base case for anyone else is worse.
“Somebody has to be beating the market — trading is a two-sided game.”
Yes — and it's worth naming who actually wins: market-makers and high-frequency firms (paid a tiny spread billions of times — a different business, not stock-picking), insiders trading their own knowledge (illegal), a small set of closed, capacity-limited quantitative funds, and the temporarily lucky. What's missing from that list is 'a person at home selecting stocks from public information.' That specific game is the one this paper measures — and it loses.
“People DO make money on options / 0DTE / picking — I've seen the screenshots.”
Both things are true at once, and §6c's first chart is the reconciliation: 75% of stocks made money over the decade (a bull market pays almost everyone) while 6.2% beat QQQ — and in options, where the average retail trade loses ~4% [25], a large minority of trades still win, and only the wins get screenshotted. 'Made money' is the wrong test: the test is against what the same dollars did in the automatic alternative, and screenshots never show that column. The right tail of any distribution is real, visible, and not a strategy.
“There must be strategies out there that work. Somewhere. No?”
Yes — and they share three properties: they're capacity-limited (they die if too much money uses them), closed or unbuyable (tolls, seats, infrastructure, control, private information — §6b), and never sold to you (a real edge is worth more traded than sold; what's sold is the story). Everything purchasable — funds, newsletters, courses, signals, ETFs — has a measured record, and it's this study. Even published academic edges decay 58% on publication [10]. The strategy that works and is available to you is the one this paper is about: own the machine and don't interrupt it.
“How often can research actually find winning stocks? Is the work worth it?”
Measured directly: the base rate of a pick beating QQQ over a year is ~42%; the best signals found in years of systematic research — insider clusters, quality screens, machine learning over 36 feature types — lift that to ~44–48%. Never past the coin flip. So the honest wage of stock research is: hundreds of hours buys ~3–6 percentage points of hit rate, landing you still below 50/50 against the free alternative — before costs and taxes take their share. As work, it pays negatively; as a hobby, price it like one (11.4). The exception that CAN pay is §6c's error-structure requirement: knowledge that isn't in anyone else's model — which comes from your profession, not from research tools everyone owns.

“But my approach would work…”

“I'll just buy NVIDIA / Apple / the obvious winner.”
Today's obvious winner is obvious because it already won — you can no longer buy the past returns. Buying today's hottest stocks and holding was tested directly on point-in-time data: the 20 hottest stocks of 2015, held to 2026, turned $20k into $53k while QQQ turned it into $160k — and 10 of the 20 no longer exist (§4). And spotting the next one early doesn't solve it: the historical winners fell −67% to −87% on the way to their gains (§4). Almost nobody holds a single stock through an −87% loss; the index made you hold, automatically.
“Momentum investing is proven by academics.”
Momentum is real as a relative pattern — recent winners beat recent losers on average, for a while. But converting that into beating QQQ requires constant rotation, and every rotating version tested here failed the audits (luck-level results, era-dependence, trade-day sensitivity — §5), while the buy-and-hold version is a disaster (§4). QQQ is itself a free, automatic momentum machine: winners grow their own weight with zero trades and zero taxes.
“Value investing: buy good companies when they're cheap.”
Tested as a systematic rule (buy statistically cheap/quality names), it produced 0.60× QQQ's result. The deep problem: for the last two decades the market's gains came overwhelmingly from stocks that never looked cheap — a value discipline would have kept you out of almost every big winner the whole way up. Value works as a risk story across a century of data; it has not been a way to beat a winner-riding index in this one.
“Buy the dip — it always comes back.”
Two versions tested. Buying beaten-down stocks: the fallen names as a group keep underperforming (their −50%+ dips are often on the way to −100%; a quarter of all stocks lost money over a decade in which the index made +638%). Waiting in cash to buy the index after a big dip: measured directly — waiting for −20% crashes before investing ended 30% poorer than just investing every month (§7). Dips feel buyable in hindsight because the ones that recovered are the ones you remember.
“Dividend stocks pay you to wait — that's the safe way to win.”
Dividends are not extra return — the share price drops by the dividend on payment; you're taxed on it annually; and dividend-screened portfolios concentrate in slow-growth sectors that structurally lagged this index. Fine as a preference for income; not a mechanism for beating a growth index, and none of the income-tilted configurations tested came close.
“Small caps outperform over time — I'll fish in that pond.”
The most important negative result in the research: an all-knowing oracle — allowed to pick, with perfect foresight, the future best 5 stocks from the small/mid-cap pond — still lost to QQQ-DCA in 3 of 5 eras, because the pond as a whole lagged so badly that even its best swimmers couldn't compensate at portfolio scale. If perfection loses in that pond, no signal wins in it.
“Equal-weighting or rebalancing my picks reduces risk and adds return.”
Every act of trimming winners and topping up losers moves money from the stocks driving all the gains into the ones that aren't. In a market where the top 1% of stocks produce ~19% of all net gains and the top 10% produce ~61% (§3), owning less of the winners is usually fatal. Tested: equal-weight and profit-taking variants underperformed both their let-it-ride versions and the index.
“Sell covered calls / puts for extra income.”
Stock returns are extremely right-skewed: nearly all the money is in a few huge upward runs (§3). A covered call sells exactly that upside — for a small premium. It converts the one part of the distribution that pays into income today, which is why long-run covered-call indices lag their underlying. Income strategies feel good monthly and cost you the decade.
“I'll copy Congress members / famous investors / whale trackers.”
Disclosures arrive with a lag (up to 45 days for both Congress and 13F filings), the copied portfolios are stale by the time you see them, and the copy-trackers' live results — where honestly measured — fail the same audits (era-dependence, luck-range results). The information you can copy is, by construction, information the market has already had weeks to price.
“Buy IPOs — get in on the ground floor of the next Amazon.”
Measured on 6,599 IPOs including every failure: a published IPO system advertising 20.5%/yr delivered 8.1%/yr when rebuilt on honest data — below the index. Most IPOs underperform for years after listing; the ground floor is, on average, above fair value because insiders choose when to sell to you.
“Follow the insiders — executives buying their own stock.”
Tested with the complete filing record: insider-cluster buying is one of the better signals — it lifts the odds a pick beats QQQ by ~4–6 percentage points. But the base rate is ~40%, so the best insider-informed picks still beat the index less than half the time. An edge that real, applied honestly, still wasn't enough to overcome the pond and concentration problems.
“AI changes everything — a model can find the winners now.”
Modern machine learning was tested extensively on this exact question with clean, point-in-time data. The models genuinely can rank stocks — and still lost to QQQ, because their confident picks all cluster in the same crowded trades and fail together: measured directly, the ML's top picks landed in the following year's top performers less often than randomly chosen stocks (§5). Also: whatever a widely available AI knows is, by definition, available to everyone — and therefore already in prices.
“Didn't monkeys throwing darts beat the index?”
The famous dart-throwing-monkey portfolios (and the studies behind them) beat cap-weighted indexes historically because random picking systematically over-weights small and cheap stocks — hidden extra risk that paid off in those samples. In the recent mega-cap era that same tilt was exactly what lost (§3): our 100 random portfolios — same idea, honest modern data — ended at a median 0.58× QQQ with 6% winning. The monkeys weren't skilled; they were levered to a bet that has since stopped paying.
“What about day trading? I've seen the funded-account guys.”
The best evidence is brutal: tracking every person who began day-trading index futures in Brazil over three years, 97% of those who stuck with it 300+ days lost money, only 1.1% out-earned minimum wage, and performance did not improve with experience [16]. U.S. brokerage data points the same way: the most active retail traders lagged the market by 6.5 points a year [14]. Day trading is the picking problem of this paper, repeated hundreds of times a year at maximum cost.
“Why not just buy the Magnificent 7 / today's giants?”
Because today's giants are the previous game's winners, and the crown is heavy. We measured it directly: take the 10 most-dominant U.S. mega-caps at each of 2000, 2005, 2010 and 2015 and hold each group ten years — only 11 of the 40 beat QQQ. The 2000 giants (Cisco, Intel, Microsoft…) lost more than the crashing index; the 2005 and 2010 cohorts won 2-of-10 and 1-of-10; even the 2015 cohort — which included Meta, Netflix and Microsoft at the dawn of the mega-cap decade — went only 5-for-10. Published research finds the same 'top dog curse' in every market studied [24]. The index rode the Mag-7 up without you guessing them, and when leadership rotates, it will ride the next set too — automatically. A hand-held Mag-7 basket won't.

“Technical analysis works — support, resistance, moving averages, patterns.”
This is the single most-tested claim in finance, with the clearest verdict. Sullivan, Timmermann & White tested 7,846 technical trading rules over 100 years of Dow data with statistics that account for trying many rules: the apparent winners had no genuine predictive power out-of-sample, and nothing worked at all on more recent data [19]. A follow-up on historical claims of chart-pattern success found they dissolve once you correct for having searched thousands of rules [20]. Charts describe the past beautifully; a century of testing says they do not predict the future — and our own trend-rule tests (§7: results that flip on the trade date) are the same finding in miniature.
“Sell in May / the January effect / seasonal patterns.”
Calendar effects were tested the same rigorous way — every seasonal rule anyone had proposed, adjusted for the fact that thousands were searched: none survive [21]. The seasonal patterns you read about are what randomness looks like when you sort it by month, and the famous ones stopped 'working' around the time they were published — the standard fate of published patterns (−58% after publication [10]).
“QQQ is obviously overvalued right now — I'll wait for a better price.”
This is market timing wearing a valuation costume, and it has a measured record: valuation-based timing signals (like CAPE) have been persistently 'expensive' for most of the last two decades — a timer following them sat out most of the gains. The definitive study is titled, accurately, Market Timing: Sin a Little — even its sympathetic authors found valuation timing adds almost nothing beyond simply staying invested [22]. Our §7 shows the local version: waiting for the obvious discount cost ~30% of final wealth. Valuation tells you expected returns are lower than usual; it does not tell you when, or from what higher level, the repricing comes.
“I'll buy call options / LEAPS / 0DTE for leveraged upside.”
The complete record of retail options trading is one of the most one-sided in finance: in comprehensive U.S. brokerage data, retail option purchases lose ~4% per trade on average, and the now-dominant same-day-expiry (0DTE) trades do several points worse [25]; an earlier study of a full brokerage's clients concluded most were, in effect, gambling and 'incurred large losses' [26]. Options add three costs stocks don't have — time decay, volatility overpricing, and wide spreads — so even a correct directional view usually loses money on the position. If §3 showed stock-picking is a lottery, bought options are the same lottery with an expiry date.
“Fine, no stock picking — I'll buy the momentum/value factor ETF instead.”
Factor ETFs are the index-fund version of the strategies in §5, and they inherit the same fate plus fees: published factor premia decay ~58% after publication [10], most factor ETFs launched after the factor's famous decade, and none of the major U.S. factor ETFs (momentum, value, quality, low-vol) has beaten QQQ over its life. Specialized and thematic ETFs are worse — they launch at peak hype and underperform by ~6%/yr over their first five years, losing ~30% risk-adjusted [23]. An ETF wrapper changes the fees, not the arithmetic.
“I'll use stop-losses so my downside is capped.”
A stop-loss converts a temporary decline into a permanent exit — and §4.3 showed the era's best stocks fell −67% to −87% on the way to their legendary gains; any stop tight enough to 'protect' you guarantees you sell every future winner during its ordinary crashes. On the index itself, stop-and-re-enter rules are just trend-timing, which failed the §7 audits (results that depend on the trade date). The honest way to cap downside is position size decided in advance (11.4), not an automated promise to sell low.
“My newsletter/analyst has a great track record.”
The oldest result in empirical finance, from 1933: Alfred Cowles collected ~12,000 professional forecasts and asked Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast? — his abstract answer: 'It is doubtful' [27]. Sixty years later, Graham & Harvey graded 326 investment newsletters over 13 years: no evidence of any timing ability as a group, and the hot ones didn't stay hot [28]. Combine that with §6's luck math (track records arise by chance in the thousands) and the five audits (§5): a track record you're shown is marketing until it survives the audits — and in 90+ years of checking, essentially none have.

“But the test itself is flawed…”

“Is this just a U.S. / QQQ phenomenon?”
No — the lottery structure is global. Bessembinder's worldwide follow-up (64,000+ stocks across 42 countries, 1990–2018): the top 1.3% of firms produced ALL $44.7 trillion of global net wealth creation; outside the U.S., less than 1% of firms did — and 61% of non-U.S. stocks returned less than U.S. Treasury bills [31]. SPIVA runs the professional scorecard in every region — Europe, Japan, Australia, India — with the same shape of result as the U.S. The mechanisms in this study (skew, concentration, the arithmetic of active management) are properties of equity markets, not of one country or one index.
“Backtests aren't real life.”
Correct — real life is worse for the picker. These simulations already charge trading costs and use only information available at each decision date, but they can't charge you the behavioral tax: real investors panic-sell in crashes and chase after rallies, which studies of actual investor returns put at another 1–2%/yr of loss. Every gap between simulation and reality widens the index's lead.
“QQQ just got lucky — tech happened to win this era.”
Partly true — §2.2 gives this objection a full four-step answer, and the paper concedes the era-dependence openly (§9): QQQ-DCA is a concentrated bet that suffered −81% in the dot-com crash. But the anti-picking verdict does not depend on QQQ: pickers failed against SPY too, professionals fail against every benchmark (SPIVA), and the skew/concentration math (§3) holds in every equity market ever measured. Choose a broader index if you prefer — just don't hand-pick stocks against it.
“If everyone indexes, markets break and picking will work again.”
A theoretical limit that is nowhere near binding: trillions still trade actively every day, and price discovery needs remarkably little active money. If indexing ever did create big, easy mispricings, the professional funds already armed and paid to exploit them would harvest them long before a retail picker could. You don't need to volunteer to be the market's price-setter.
“Isn't QQQ itself just someone's stock picks?”
It's a rule, not a judgment: the 100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies, weighted by size, membership refreshed mechanically. Nobody decides 'this company will win'; the weighting means whatever wins automatically becomes a bigger holding, and whatever dies falls out on its own. That mechanical winner-riding — free, tax-deferred, emotionless — is precisely the thing discretionary picking keeps failing to replicate.
“A 10-year backtest window is cherry-picked.”
The core statistics were measured across every era in 26 years of data, including both crashes: the fraction of stocks beating QQQ was computed for each year 2000–2025 (average 42%, §3), the winner-persistence numbers span 24 annual cohorts, and the strategy tests were run and re-run across eras, both halves of the sample, and dozens of start dates. The conclusion is the same in every honest slice — with one telling exception: most stocks 'beat QQQ' in 2000–2001, when QQQ itself was collapsing. Beating the index is easiest when the index is losing.

Practical questions

“Should I DCA or invest a lump sum?”
If you already have a lump sum, history favors investing it immediately about two times in three (markets rise more often than not, so waiting has a cost). DCA in this paper means the thing almost everyone actually does: investing money as it's earned, every paycheck. The point is not the cadence — biweekly vs monthly was tested and is a rounding error — it's the automation: no decisions, no timing, no picks.
“What about gold, crypto, real estate, international stocks?”
Different question. Those are asset-allocation choices — how much risk, of which kinds, you want to hold — and diversification genuinely smooths the ride (at some cost to return). This paper is about one specific claim: that you can select stocks to beat a stock index. Nothing here argues against owning other assets; everything here argues against paying anyone (including yourself) to pick stocks.
“Can I pick a few stocks just for fun?”
Yes — with a budget. Carve out a small fixed slice (say 5%), call it entertainment, and measure it honestly against what the same dollars would have done in QQQ. Expect it to lag (that's what the evidence says it will do), enjoy it like any hobby with a cost, and never let it grow into the plan. The danger isn't the 5% — it's the promotion of a lucky year into a strategy.
“What would it actually take to beat the index?”
The research measured this precisely: surprisingly little skill — if the errors are independent. A simulated picker with only a slight, honest edge whose mistakes were random beat QQQ in every era. What no tested signal achieved is that error-independence: every public-data signal's bold picks are the same crowded bet in different tickers, so they fail together. Genuinely independent edges come from information about individual companies that others don't have — which, for a private individual trading public information, is either unavailable or illegal to use.

11 · The playbook: exactly what to do

Evidence without instructions is trivia. Here is the complete, quantified playbook this research supports — including honest answers to "how much?", "what about picking a few stocks anyway?", and "when do I sell?".

11.1  First, the order of operations (before any investing)

  • Cash buffer first: 3–6 months of expenses. Its job is to make sure you are never forced to sell stocks in a crash.
  • Free money second: any employer retirement match, always, fully.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts before taxable (401k/IRA equivalents): the same QQQ-DCA compounds meaningfully faster untaxed.
  • Only money you won't need for 10+ years goes into equities. The index fell −81% once (§9) and −32% on a DCA account twice in 20 years. Money you'll need within ~5 years doesn't belong in it — being forced to sell into a crash is the one unrecoverable mistake.

11.2  How much goes into QQQ-DCA? All of your long-term money — if you pass the stomach test

This study's answer is simple: every dollar you are investing for 10+ years goes into the automatic QQQ purchase. Not because QQQ can't fall — but because every tested attempt to improve on it (picking, timing, rotating, waiting) ended with less money. There is no clever remainder to allocate; the schedule IS the strategy.

The honest price of that answer, quantified, so you can pre-commit with open eyes:

  • A steady QQQ-DCA account fell −32% from its peak twice in 20 years (2008–09, 2022) — and kept buying. Both times the automatic contributions bought the bottom, and the account went on to new highs. The people who lost were the ones who stopped.
  • The worst case on record is worse: a lump sum at the March-2000 top fell −81% and took ~15 years to break even (§9). A contributor through that same disaster recovered far sooner and ended far ahead — steady buying through the crash is precisely what repairs it (§2's table starts at that worst possible moment and still ends at 16× the money in).
  • So the sizing question is not a percentage — it's this stomach test: when (not if) the account shows −30%, will you keep the automation on? If yes: all long-term money, full weight. If honestly no: automate the largest amount for which the answer is yes — a plan you hold at the bottom beats a bigger plan you abandon there (the measured cost of self-inflicted timing is 1–2 points a year [15], and far worse when it happens inside a crash).
  • Money needed within ~5 years stays out entirely (11.1). Five-to-ten-year money: partial, scaled to how certain the need is.
The stomach test, drawn: a QQQ-DCA account's distance below its own peak, 2000–2026. Every red valley was a moment the plan felt broken; every one repaired by continued buying.

Should you "always and only" do this? Always — automate it and never override it; every override pathway was measured above and lost. Only — yes, for stock-market money: this entire study is the evidence that adding picking, timing, or rotation on top subtracts. The one sanctioned exception is the capped hobby slice in 11.4.

11.3  “But people made fortunes on Apple and Tesla — why can't I?”

Because you are looking at the winners of a lottery and asking why you can't buy winning tickets. The odds, measured over 21 years (every investable U.S. stock in 2005, followed to 2026, deaths included — 1,141 stocks):

What a single randomly-chosen 2005 stock did over 21 years. QQQ over the same period: 24×.
  • 1 in 5 single-stock tickets lost money over two decades of a rising market. The median ticket made 3.2× while QQQ made 24× — so the typical stock pick trailed the index by a factor of ~7.
  • 1 in 6 made 10× — respectable, still less than the index's 24×.
  • 1 in 22 beat the index at all. 1 in ~560 (0.18%) was an Apple-class 100-bagger.
  • And drawing the golden ticket is not enough: you then had to size it large, hold it through −67% to −87% collapses (§4.3) — several times — and never take profits after the first 10×. Each requirement eliminates almost everyone who starts. That triple filter — rare ticket × meaningful size × inhuman holding — is why the Apple stories are famous: famous is what nearly-impossible looks like from the outside.

Meanwhile the index holder got the Apple outcome anyway — at the index's weight, automatically, with none of the decisions. That is the quiet punchline of this entire paper: owning QQQ IS owning Apple, Nvidia and Tesla — at full size, without needing to be right.

11.4  If you're going to pick stocks anyway: the satellite rules

Realistically, some readers will pick stocks regardless. Fine — do it in a way that caps the damage and keeps the compounding machine intact. The quantified rules:

  • Cap the slice at 5–10% of your portfolio, hard. The measured cost: portfolios of hand-picked stocks trailed QQQ by ≈4.6%/yr (median of 100 random-pick portfolios; skilled-looking signals did no better after audits). Over 20 years that lag costs:

  • Buy once or DCA in — doesn't much matter; the cap is what matters. A satellite position's fate is dominated by which stock it is, not how you entered it.
  • What to look for (measured, not vibes): the only signals that tested positive were boring ones — sustained insider cluster buying (+4–6 points to the odds) and steady profitable-quality names. What tested worst is exactly what feels best: the 12-month leaderboard (5–17% forward odds), story stocks, and whatever is on your feed. If your pick is exciting, that's a warning, not a signal.
  • Diversify the slice across 8–10 names — or accept that 1–3 names is a lottery ticket (§11.3 odds) and size it like one.
  • Never average down. "It's cheaper now" is how 20% of stocks ride to zero. The measured fate of falling former winners: 56% land below the median stock the next year. Adding to losers is moving money in the exact opposite direction of everything §3 showed.
  • Selling rules, decided in advance: the QQQ core is never sold (that's the whole edge). A satellite winner is left alone until it outgrows your cap — then trimmed into the core (winner → index-of-winners; never into your losers). A satellite loser needs no decision: it simply never gets another dollar, and it dies or lives on its own. This preserves the let-winners-run principle at every level while capping single-stock risk. (Deciding in advance matters because instinct is measurably backwards: real investors sell their winners ~50% more readily than their losers — the “disposition effect” [32] — the exact opposite of what §3 rewards.)
  • Measure it honestly once a year against what the same dollars in QQQ did. The evidence says it will lag; when it does, you'll have paid a known, capped price for the fun — and when a pick 10×'s, you'll enjoy it without having bet the plan on it.

Study that chart carefully, because it contains the whole lesson:

  • Solar (TAN): the theme was completely right — solar deployment grew roughly a hundred-fold — and the fund still lost money over 18 years while QQQ made 28×. Being right about the future is not the same trade as making money, because the future was already in the price, competition destroyed the margins, and the winners were companies that didn't exist yet.
  • Innovation (ARKK): the era's most famous future-picking fund: half of QQQ's return with a −75% crash.
  • Biotech (XBI): the genomics revolution happened; the fund made half the index.
  • Semiconductors (SMH): the one theme that won — and notice which one: not the exciting new story but the boring incumbent toolmakers that every theme has to buy from (and you had to hold through −52%). That's the only theme pattern with a winning record: picks-and-shovels incumbents, not frontier stories.

If you're going to hunt anyway, the protocol (each rule maps to a measured failure above):

  • Keep it inside the 5–10% satellite (11.4) — sized by the Kelly logic for a negative-expectancy bet: the measured edge of picking is negative, so the "optimal" aggressive size is zero and anything you allocate is priced as entertainment/tuition. The cap converts a plan-killer into a hobby with a known cost (−3% to −6% of lifetime wealth).
  • Hunt where the required edge could actually exist (§6c): your own professional domain — the industry you genuinely know better than analysts covering 40 companies. That is the only legal channel for the uncrowded, company-specific information the math requires. If your idea comes from a feed, a ranking, or a theme ETF launch, it is by definition crowded — the measured odds of those were 5–17%.
  • Prefer the boring implementation of the trend — the SMH pattern: incumbent suppliers with profits today, not stories with promises. Wait out the hype phase: theme funds launch at peak excitement and shed ~6%/yr for five years after [23]; the survivors are still there after the crash.
  • Buy 8–10 tickets, equal-sized, and hold five-plus years. Here's the striking arithmetic: with the measured 17% chance each pick 10×'s over two decades, holding 8 tickets gives you a 77% chance of owning at least one 10-bagger — and still only a ~6% chance the basket beats QQQ. Both at once. You will very likely get a trophy and lose the race — which is exactly why everyone knows someone with a great pick and almost no one who beat the index. Decide in advance which you're playing for.
  • Expectations, measured: a 90/10 core-satellite (satellite performing at the measured picker median) ends a decade at ≈96% of pure QQQ-DCA wealth; a lucky (90th-percentile) satellite gets you to ≈99%; an unlucky one ≈94%. The satellite decides your stories; the core decides your wealth.
  • Review yearly against QQQ; feed winners' trims to the core; never feed losers. (11.4's rules apply unchanged.)

11.7  The do-not list (each one measured somewhere above)

  • ✗ No waiting in cash for crashes (§7: cost ~30% of final wealth).
  • ✗ No on/off market timing switches (§7: trade-day lottery).
  • ✗ No leverage you haven't priced at −84% (§8).
  • ✗ No selling winners to buy losers, anywhere, ever (§3–4).
  • ✗ No strategies sold on a backtest that hasn't passed the five audits (§5).
  • ✗ No acting on streaks — yours or anyone's (§6: three 10-year streaks per 10,000 coin-flippers).
How to prove this study wrong. A real study states its falsification conditions. This one is wrong if any of the following is produced: (1) a rule-based strategy, specified in advance, that beats QQQ-DCA on point-in-time delisting-inclusive data and passes all five audits (random-picker null, lead-timing, other eras, other trade days, honest costs); (2) a picker or fund, open to ordinary investors at ordinary size, with 15+ years of audited live returns above QQQ net of fees — enough to clear the skill-detection bar in §6c; or (3) a demonstration that the base-rate measurements here (6.2%, 42%, 4.6%, 0.18%) are materially wrong on equivalent data. We checked every candidate we could find, including our own. The section stays until someone clears it — and the authors would genuinely like to see it cleared.

Methodology, references & sources

Limitations & robustness (stated plainly)

  • Delisting convention: headline decade stats count disappeared stocks at their final traded price (acquisitions exit at deal price). Under the harshest alternative (every disappearance = −100%), the share of stocks beating QQQ moves from 6.2% to 5.9% — the conclusion is insensitive to the convention.
  • Window robustness: the core result was measured three independent ways — one full decade (2016–26: 6.2% beat), twenty-six annual cohorts (2000–25: average 42% beat over 12 months, majority-beat only when QQQ itself was crashing), and a 21-year lottery (2005–26: 4.6% beat). All agree.
  • Granularity: stock-level analysis is monthly; it cannot see intraday effects (which the day-trading literature covers, and which are worse for retail [14][16][25]).
  • Scope: U.S. common stocks only; QQQ measured as the actual ETF (fees included). Taxes are not modeled — omitting them flatters every challenger, since the challengers trade and the benchmark doesn't.
  • The era caveat is §9's: 1999–2026 is one long sample dominated by U.S. large-cap tech; the anti-picking mechanisms (skew, concentration, arithmetic of active management) are era-independent, but QQQ's specific margin over broader indexes is not.
  • Simulations are simulations: every number here is a backtest or historical measurement, not a guarantee; the behavioral evidence [14][15][16] suggests live results for active approaches would be worse, not better.
