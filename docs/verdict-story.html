Chapter 01The rules of the test

First, we make cheating impossible.

The question: investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule (every two weeks or monthly — both tested, the difference is negligible), can any rule for choosing stocks or timing purchases end with more money than putting every contribution into QQQ? Three rules make the test honest — and they matter more than any strategy.

🕰️  Point-in-time data

Every simulated decision uses only information that existed on that day. No hindsight. Ever.

💀  The dead are counted

The data includes every stock that later went bankrupt, was delisted, or was acquired — at its real final price. Most "market-beating" backtests you'll see online quietly use only companies that still exist today, which is like studying lottery winners to conclude lotteries pay well.

Why a top-100 index keeps beating the broader 500

Mechanically, QQQ is a purer expression of the one force this whole story documents: letting winners run at full weight. Fewer names means the mega-winners are a larger share; size-weighting means their weight grows automatically as they win. No judgment is involved — just less dilution of the lottery tickets that Chapter 3 shows produce everything.

"But choosing QQQ as the benchmark is itself hindsight — isn't this whole study circular?"

The sharpest objection to this study, so it gets a full answer. Separate two claims that are usually blurred together:

Claim A (benchmark-independent): no method of selecting stocks or timing purchases reliably beats an automatic, cap-weighted index — whichever index you choose.

Claim B (benchmark-specific): QQQ has been the better index to automate this era, and choosing it going forward is a deliberate, concentrated bet.

The verdict rests entirely on Claim A. Claim B only decides which machine you automate — and Chapter 9 states its risk openly.

Step 1 — Swap the benchmark; the verdict survives
If "nothing beats QQQ" were true only because QQQ ran hot, pickers would beat a cooler benchmark. They don't: the same 100 random 10-stock portfolios that ended at a median 0.58× of QQQ-DCA also lost to SPY-DCA — median 0.84×, with two-thirds trailing the broader index too. And the professional scorecard (SPIVA) doesn't use QQQ at all: it measures every fund against its own category benchmark — value funds vs value indexes, small-cap funds vs small-cap indexes — and finds ~90% failure everywhere over 15 years [12]. A conclusion that survives substituting the benchmark is, by definition, not benchmark selection bias; the choice of QQQ changes the size of the picker's shortfall, not its existence.
Step 2 — The core mechanism is an identity, not a sample
For any index over any universe, the average actively-managed dollar in that universe must equal the index before costs and trail it after costs (Sharpe's arithmetic [7]) — true in 1929, true in 2000, true if technology collapses tomorrow. And the skew that makes concentrated picking worse than average is measured across a century of U.S. data [1] and 42 countries [31]. Neither premise mentions QQQ.
Step 3 — Even perfect benchmark hindsight doesn't rescue picking
Grant the picker our exact "bias": tell them in 2015 that U.S. mega-cap tech will win. Picking inside the winning pond still lost — the era's dominant giants beat QQQ only 11/40 times across four decade-cohorts; momentum strategies restricted to the index's own members died in audit; the tech-theme funds trailed (Chapter 11). The benchmark's era explains the benchmark's return; it does not create the picker's shortfall. The shortfall comes from skew + costs, which follow the picker into any pond.
Step 4 — The rule predates the result
QQQ is not a basket assembled after seeing what won — the Nasdaq-100 rule dates to 1985 and the ETF to 1999, before every window measured here. Contrast the actual hindsight construction people confuse it with: "the Magnificent 7" is a list defined by past winning, and Chapter 10b shows what buying such lists ex-ante actually did. This study never benchmarks against anything defined by the outcome.

QQQ and SPY are two overlapping winner-riding machines: SPY also held Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia — at somewhat smaller weights. Choose the "wrong" one and you still capture the winning tail automatically; the maximum regret over 26 years was a factor of ~2, while still multiplying your money ~8×. Stock selection is the opposite object: a repeated game — hundreds of decisions, each with measured sub-50% odds, costs compounding — with unbounded regret: the median pick captures a quarter of the index's decade and the tail outcome is zero. Choosing between trains headed the same direction is not the same kind of decision as betting you can outrun them. That is why this story agonizes over stock-picking and treats QQQ-vs-SPY as a one-paragraph preference.

Now the logic, alternative by alternative — because the record alone would be recency, and the circularity rules above apply to us too:

Total-market funds (VTI-style)
A cap-weighted total-market fund is ~85% the S&P 500 by weight — the small-cap tail it adds behaved like the ponds in Chapter 5 (an oracle picking small-caps perfectly still lost). It is the same machine, slightly diluted; choose it or SPY interchangeably. Nothing in this study changes.
International (EAFE, emerging)
Two facts and one concession. Fact one: the two-decade record above — roughly a quarter of QQQ's outcome at equal or worse drawdowns. Fact two, the structural one: you already own the world through U.S. listings — the S&P 500 earns roughly 40% of its revenue abroad, and the world's dominant companies overwhelmingly choose U.S. listings; meanwhile the no-view global index (VT-style) is itself ~60–65% U.S. by cap-weight, so "maximum humility" moves your weights less than it sounds. The concession: Claim A holds within every market (the skew is global [31]) — if you genuinely prefer world weights, automate a global cap-weighted fund and every conclusion here still applies. What's indefensible isn't the region — it's picking and timing inside it.
Japan — the strongest warning in market history, faced directly
The Nikkei's 1989 peak took about three decades to reclaim. That is what a single-country machine bought at bubble prices can do, and no honest study waves it away. Three replies, not one: (i) that catastrophe was a lump sum at the top of one market — a steady contributor kept buying Japan's bottoms for decades and recovered years earlier (the identical mechanism Chapter 11.2 shows on QQQ's own −81%); (ii) it is the case for broad, multi-market cap-weighting if you fear it — not for stock-picking, which the circularity steps above showed fails within Japan too; (iii) today's QQQ concentration is the closest modern rhyme to 1989 Tokyo — which is exactly why Chapter 9 exists and why Claim B is labeled a bet, not a law.
A sector fund as the core
The cap-weighted index already is a sector rotator — over 50 years its internal weights migrated from industrials and energy to technology automatically, with what looks like perfect hindsight because it requires no foresight. Committing to one sector permanently is a pond bet plus rotation risk: Chapter 11.5 measured the fate of the reigning #1 sector — below the sector median 59% of the time the following year. And if your sector conviction is specifically "technology keeps leading" — that conviction is QQQ, expressed with 100 companies of internal diversification instead of 25.
Gold
Honesty first — over this exact window gold out-compounded every international equity fund above (a strong decade at each end), and it crashes differently than stocks (−27% worst here). But it is not a compounding machine and cannot be one: gold has no earnings, no cash flows, and no growth engine — every dollar of its rise is repricing, not production, and its multi-century real return is near zero. It captured 41% of QQQ's result in its own good era; over horizons where compounding dominates, the gap widens without bound. As crisis insurance it's a separate, legitimate question this paper doesn't cover; as the engine, holding gold is conceding the engine.

The pattern across all five: every alternative is either the same machine in a different wrapper (total-market, global cap-weight), a bet this study already prices (sector = pond + rotation), or a different asset class doing a different job (gold). None of them changes the verdict on picking and timing — and the one that's genuinely defensible on humility grounds (global cap-weighting) is defensible precisely because it, too, is an automatic winner-riding rule.

The published record says the same thing

Bessembinder (2018), studying every U.S. stock since 1926 (~26,000 companies): the best-performing 4% of companies account for the stock market's entire net gain over Treasury bills; the other 96% collectively matched cash. Four out of seven stocks returned less than Treasury bills over their lifetime, and just five companies produced 10% of all wealth ever created [1]. His global follow-up found it's even more extreme worldwide.

J.P. Morgan's "Agony & Ecstasy" study (Russell 3000, 1980–2014): 40% of all stocks suffered a catastrophic decline of 70%+ from which they never recovered; two-thirds underperformed the index; the median stock lagged it by −54% over its lifetime [2].

Heaton, Polson & Witte, "Why Indexing Works" (2017) formalized the mechanism: when a few stocks drive everything, any subset you pick most likely misses them, so most pickers must trail the index — before costs [3].

It's not one lucky decade. Year by year since 2000, the fraction of stocks beating QQQ over the following 12 months averaged 42% — and the years it exceeded 50% are mostly 2000–2001, when "beating QQQ" meant falling less than an index that was crashing:

% of stocks beating QQQ over the next 12 months, each year 2000–2025
Dashed line = coin flip. The picker's game has been sub-50/50 in almost every normal year.
This is the whole game. Picking stocks means trading away guaranteed full-size ownership of the few tickets that pay, for a handful of tickets that each probably won't. The index isn't smart — it just refuses to hand any winning ticket back.

The published record

The same non-persistence holds for professionals: S&P's Persistence Scorecard finds that of funds in the top quartile in any year, almost none remain top-quartile a few years later — at rates at or below what chance predicts [4]. Momentum itself is a real, Nobel-adjacent finding (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993 [5]) — but it's a short-horizon relative effect that decays and periodically crashes (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016 [6]); it is not "good stocks keep being good."

The published record

Sharpe's "Arithmetic of Active Management" (1991) is the iron law underneath all of this: the average actively-managed dollar must earn the market return before costs, and less than it after costs — always, by accounting identity. Beating the index is a zero-sum game played against professionals, minus fees [7].

Fama & French, "Luck versus Skill" (2010): across 3,000+ mutual funds, so few beat their benchmarks net of costs that the winners are statistically indistinguishable from what luck alone would produce [8]. Carhart (1997) found the same decades earlier: no persistent fund skill beyond fees and momentum exposure [9].

McLean & Pontiff (2016): even published academic anomalies lose ~26% of their returns after their sample period ends and ~58% after publication — edges die on contact with daylight [10]. Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) showed most published factors likely aren't real at all once you correct for how many were tested — the same multiple-testing trap our audits caught [11].

The published record

SPIVA (S&P, year-end 2024): 89.5% of professional U.S. large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years — full-time managers, with research teams, before your tax disadvantages [12]. Buffett's famous 10-year bet (2008–2017): a plain S&P 500 index fund returned +125.8%; the hand-picked hedge-fund portfolios averaged roughly +36% — he donated the winnings and repeated the advice: index [13].

And for the individual actually doing the trading: Barber & Odean (2000), 66,465 real brokerage households — the most active traders earned 11.4%/yr while the market returned 17.9% [14]. Morningstar's "Mind the Gap": real investors earn ~1.1 percentage points per year less than the very funds they hold, from timing their entries and exits [15]. A study of every Brazilian who began day-trading index futures (2013–2015): of those who persisted 300+ days, 97% lost money, and only 1.1% earned more than minimum wage [16].

Chapter 06·bSo who actually DOES outperform?

Markets aren't unbeatable. They're unbeatable from where you're sitting.

The honest census of who wins, and the mechanism each uses. Notice what every entry has in common: none of them is "a person at home selecting stocks from public information."

🏦 Market-makers and high-frequency firms

They don't predict anything; they sell liquidity, collecting a fraction of a cent on billions of trades (including yours). It's a toll booth, not a forecast — and it requires infrastructure you cannot rent.

🔒 A handful of closed quantitative funds

The famous one returns ~66%/yr gross — thousands of tiny, fast statistical edges, capacity-capped at a few billion dollars, closed to outside money for decades. The same firm's funds that outsiders can buy have performed near the market. Capacity is the tell: real edges are small; anything sold to unlimited money isn't one.

🛠️ Activists and private equity

They buy control and change the company. The return comes from doing, not picking.

🕵️ Corporate insiders

The one real information edge — which is why trading on it is illegal (and why the legal shadow of it, cluster buying, was the best signal we measured).

🔬 Specialists in tiny, uncrowded niches

Genuinely possible, capacity-limited, and a full-time job with a measured failure rate — not a strategy you buy.

🍀 The lucky

Chapter 6. By far the largest group, and indistinguishable from the skilled for about a decade (see 6c).

Correcting both removes ~5–6 points of reported return per year [29]; corrected aggregate returns came out at 9.29%/yr vs the S&P's 9.38% over the classic study window [29].

  • And the few genuinely great funds? See the census above: closed, capacity-capped, infrastructure businesses. The market-beating that exists is precisely the kind you cannot buy.

Above 1× = QQQ ahead. Leadership rotates: QQQ spent roughly a decade below the line before its era — the next rotation is this chapter's whole point.
  • It does not say markets are perfectly efficient — only that the specific game of out-picking a winner-riding index using public information is stacked, measurably, against the picker.
  • It does not cover other asset classes. This is a study of stock strategies measured against QQQ; whether you hold anything besides stocks is a separate question it deliberately doesn't answer.
  • It does not promise the next 26 years look like the last. It says: whatever index you choose, no tested method of picking stocks against it has honestly beaten contributing to it on autopilot.

Chapter 10·bEvery objection we could find, answered

Forty objections. Open any one.

This is the complete objection file — every counter-argument we could find, each answered with the measured evidence above. If yours is missing, that's a defect in the study.

“But people do beat the market…”
“My friend / a guy on YouTube beats the market every year.”
Given how many people pick stocks, thousands of market-beaters must exist by pure chance. A single concentrated portfolio beats QQQ in a given year roughly 40–45% of the time (measured, Ch. 3). Start 10,000 people flipping that coin and after 5 years about 185 of them have a perfect streak — after 10 years, 3 still do — with zero skill involved (chart in Ch. 6). The lucky ones post; the other 9,800 don't. You only ever hear from the right tail.
“Warren Buffett beat the market for decades.”
He did — mostly in the 1950s–1990s, buying tiny neglected companies and whole private businesses, using cheap insurance-float leverage: a game a retail stock-picker cannot play. Over roughly the last two decades Berkshire has performed about in line with the S&P 500. Buffett himself won a famous 10-year, million-dollar bet that an index fund would beat a hand-picked group of hedge funds — and his standing advice for ordinary investors is a low-cost index fund.
“Renaissance's Medallion fund makes 60%+ a year.”
True, and instructive: Medallion is capped at roughly $10–15B (returns vanish at scale), closed to outside money for decades, and earns its returns from thousands of tiny, short-lived statistical edges executed with elite infrastructure — not from 'picking good stocks.' The same firm's funds that are open to outsiders have performed near the market. The existence of a closed, capacity-limited machine says nothing about what an individual can do in a brokerage account.
“Professionals with Bloomberg terminals and armies of analysts must beat it.”
The public scorecard says the opposite: S&P's SPIVA reports have shown, year after year, that 89.5% of professional U.S. large-cap funds lagged the S&P 500 over the 15 years through 2024 [12] — after fees, with every resource money can buy. The minority who lead in one period are not reliably the same ones who lead in the next (persistence studies). If the professionals can't, the base case for anyone else is worse.
“Somebody has to be beating the market — trading is a two-sided game.”
Yes — and it's worth naming who actually wins: market-makers and high-frequency firms (paid a tiny spread billions of times — a different business, not stock-picking), insiders trading their own knowledge (illegal), a small set of closed, capacity-limited quantitative funds, and the temporarily lucky. What's missing from that list is 'a person at home selecting stocks from public information.' That specific game is the one this paper measures — and it loses.
“People DO make money on options / 0DTE / picking — I've seen the screenshots.”
Both things are true at once, and Ch. 6c's first chart is the reconciliation: 75% of stocks made money over the decade (a bull market pays almost everyone) while 6.2% beat QQQ — and in options, where the average retail trade loses ~4% [25], a large minority of trades still win, and only the wins get screenshotted. 'Made money' is the wrong test: the test is against what the same dollars did in the automatic alternative, and screenshots never show that column. The right tail of any distribution is real, visible, and not a strategy.
“There must be strategies out there that work. Somewhere. No?”
Yes — and they share three properties: they're capacity-limited (they die if too much money uses them), closed or unbuyable (tolls, seats, infrastructure, control, private information — Ch. 6b), and never sold to you (a real edge is worth more traded than sold; what's sold is the story). Everything purchasable — funds, newsletters, courses, signals, ETFs — has a measured record, and it's this study. Even published academic edges decay 58% on publication [10]. The strategy that works and is available to you is the one this paper is about: own the machine and don't interrupt it.
“How often can research actually find winning stocks? Is the work worth it?”
Measured directly: the base rate of a pick beating QQQ over a year is ~42%; the best signals found in years of systematic research — insider clusters, quality screens, machine learning over 36 feature types — lift that to ~44–48%. Never past the coin flip. So the honest wage of stock research is: hundreds of hours buys ~3–6 percentage points of hit rate, landing you still below 50/50 against the free alternative — before costs and taxes take their share. As work, it pays negatively; as a hobby, price it like one (11.4). The exception that CAN pay is Ch. 6c's error-structure requirement: knowledge that isn't in anyone else's model — which comes from your profession, not from research tools everyone owns.
“But my approach would work…”
“I'll just buy NVIDIA / Apple / the obvious winner.”
Today's obvious winner is obvious because it already won — you can no longer buy the past returns. Buying today's hottest stocks and holding was tested directly on point-in-time data: the 20 hottest stocks of 2015, held to 2026, turned $20k into $53k while QQQ turned it into $160k — and 10 of the 20 no longer exist (Ch. 4). And spotting the next one early doesn't solve it: the historical winners fell −67% to −87% on the way to their gains (Ch. 4). Almost nobody holds a single stock through an −87% loss; the index made you hold, automatically.
“Momentum investing is proven by academics.”
Momentum is real as a relative pattern — recent winners beat recent losers on average, for a while. But converting that into beating QQQ requires constant rotation, and every rotating version tested here failed the audits (luck-level results, era-dependence, trade-day sensitivity — Ch. 5), while the buy-and-hold version is a disaster (Ch. 4). QQQ is itself a free, automatic momentum machine: winners grow their own weight with zero trades and zero taxes.
“Value investing: buy good companies when they're cheap.”
Tested as a systematic rule (buy statistically cheap/quality names), it produced 0.60× QQQ's result. The deep problem: for the last two decades the market's gains came overwhelmingly from stocks that never looked cheap — a value discipline would have kept you out of almost every big winner the whole way up. Value works as a risk story across a century of data; it has not been a way to beat a winner-riding index in this one.
“Buy the dip — it always comes back.”
Two versions tested. Buying beaten-down stocks: the fallen names as a group keep underperforming (their −50%+ dips are often on the way to −100%; a quarter of all stocks lost money over a decade in which the index made +638%). Waiting in cash to buy the index after a big dip: measured directly — waiting for −20% crashes before investing ended 30% poorer than just investing every month (Ch. 7). Dips feel buyable in hindsight because the ones that recovered are the ones you remember.
“Dividend stocks pay you to wait — that's the safe way to win.”
Dividends are not extra return — the share price drops by the dividend on payment; you're taxed on it annually; and dividend-screened portfolios concentrate in slow-growth sectors that structurally lagged this index. Fine as a preference for income; not a mechanism for beating a growth index, and none of the income-tilted configurations tested came close.
“Small caps outperform over time — I'll fish in that pond.”
The most important negative result in the research: an all-knowing oracle — allowed to pick, with perfect foresight, the future best 5 stocks from the small/mid-cap pond — still lost to QQQ-DCA in 3 of 5 eras, because the pond as a whole lagged so badly that even its best swimmers couldn't compensate at portfolio scale. If perfection loses in that pond, no signal wins in it.
“Equal-weighting or rebalancing my picks reduces risk and adds return.”
Every act of trimming winners and topping up losers moves money from the stocks driving all the gains into the ones that aren't. In a market where the top 1% of stocks produce ~19% of all net gains and the top 10% produce ~61% (Ch. 3), owning less of the winners is usually fatal. Tested: equal-weight and profit-taking variants underperformed both their let-it-ride versions and the index.
“Sell covered calls / puts for extra income.”
Stock returns are extremely right-skewed: nearly all the money is in a few huge upward runs (Ch. 3). A covered call sells exactly that upside — for a small premium. It converts the one part of the distribution that pays into income today, which is why long-run covered-call indices lag their underlying. Income strategies feel good monthly and cost you the decade.
“I'll copy Congress members / famous investors / whale trackers.”
Disclosures arrive with a lag (up to 45 days for both Congress and 13F filings), the copied portfolios are stale by the time you see them, and the copy-trackers' live results — where honestly measured — fail the same audits (era-dependence, luck-range results). The information you can copy is, by construction, information the market has already had weeks to price.
“Buy IPOs — get in on the ground floor of the next Amazon.”
Measured on 6,599 IPOs including every failure: a published IPO system advertising 20.5%/yr delivered 8.1%/yr when rebuilt on honest data — below the index. Most IPOs underperform for years after listing; the ground floor is, on average, above fair value because insiders choose when to sell to you.
“Follow the insiders — executives buying their own stock.”
Tested with the complete filing record: insider-cluster buying is one of the better signals — it lifts the odds a pick beats QQQ by ~4–6 percentage points. But the base rate is ~40%, so the best insider-informed picks still beat the index less than half the time. An edge that real, applied honestly, still wasn't enough to overcome the pond and concentration problems.
“AI changes everything — a model can find the winners now.”
Modern machine learning was tested extensively on this exact question with clean, point-in-time data. The models genuinely can rank stocks — and still lost to QQQ, because their confident picks all cluster in the same crowded trades and fail together: measured directly, the ML's top picks landed in the following year's top performers less often than randomly chosen stocks (Ch. 5). Also: whatever a widely available AI knows is, by definition, available to everyone — and therefore already in prices.
“Didn't monkeys throwing darts beat the index?”
The famous dart-throwing-monkey portfolios (and the studies behind them) beat cap-weighted indexes historically because random picking systematically over-weights small and cheap stocks — hidden extra risk that paid off in those samples. In the recent mega-cap era that same tilt was exactly what lost (Ch. 3): our 100 random portfolios — same idea, honest modern data — ended at a median 0.58× QQQ with 6% winning. The monkeys weren't skilled; they were levered to a bet that has since stopped paying.
“What about day trading? I've seen the funded-account guys.”
The best evidence is brutal: tracking every person who began day-trading index futures in Brazil over three years, 97% of those who stuck with it 300+ days lost money, only 1.1% out-earned minimum wage, and performance did not improve with experience [16]. U.S. brokerage data points the same way: the most active retail traders lagged the market by 6.5 points a year [14]. Day trading is the picking problem of this paper, repeated hundreds of times a year at maximum cost.
“Why not just buy the Magnificent 7 / today's giants?”
Because today's giants are the previous game's winners, and the crown is heavy. We measured it directly: take the 10 most-dominant U.S. mega-caps at each of 2000, 2005, 2010 and 2015 and hold each group ten years — only 11 of the 40 beat QQQ. The 2000 giants (Cisco, Intel, Microsoft…) lost more than the crashing index; the 2005 and 2010 cohorts won 2-of-10 and 1-of-10; even the 2015 cohort — which included Meta, Netflix and Microsoft at the dawn of the mega-cap decade — went only 5-for-10. Published research finds the same 'top dog curse' in every market studied [24]. The index rode the Mag-7 up without you guessing them, and when leadership rotates, it will ride the next set too — automatically. A hand-held Mag-7 basket won't.

“Technical analysis works — support, resistance, moving averages, patterns.”
This is the single most-tested claim in finance, with the clearest verdict. Sullivan, Timmermann & White tested 7,846 technical trading rules over 100 years of Dow data with statistics that account for trying many rules: the apparent winners had no genuine predictive power out-of-sample, and nothing worked at all on more recent data [19]. A follow-up on historical claims of chart-pattern success found they dissolve once you correct for having searched thousands of rules [20]. Charts describe the past beautifully; a century of testing says they do not predict the future — and our own trend-rule tests (Ch. 7: results that flip on the trade date) are the same finding in miniature.
“Sell in May / the January effect / seasonal patterns.”
Calendar effects were tested the same rigorous way — every seasonal rule anyone had proposed, adjusted for the fact that thousands were searched: none survive [21]. The seasonal patterns you read about are what randomness looks like when you sort it by month, and the famous ones stopped 'working' around the time they were published — the standard fate of published patterns (−58% after publication [10]).
“QQQ is obviously overvalued right now — I'll wait for a better price.”
This is market timing wearing a valuation costume, and it has a measured record: valuation-based timing signals (like CAPE) have been persistently 'expensive' for most of the last two decades — a timer following them sat out most of the gains. The definitive study is titled, accurately, Market Timing: Sin a Little — even its sympathetic authors found valuation timing adds almost nothing beyond simply staying invested [22]. Our Ch. 7 shows the local version: waiting for the obvious discount cost ~30% of final wealth. Valuation tells you expected returns are lower than usual; it does not tell you when, or from what higher level, the repricing comes.
“I'll buy call options / LEAPS / 0DTE for leveraged upside.”
The complete record of retail options trading is one of the most one-sided in finance: in comprehensive U.S. brokerage data, retail option purchases lose ~4% per trade on average, and the now-dominant same-day-expiry (0DTE) trades do several points worse [25]; an earlier study of a full brokerage's clients concluded most were, in effect, gambling and 'incurred large losses' [26]. Options add three costs stocks don't have — time decay, volatility overpricing, and wide spreads — so even a correct directional view usually loses money on the position. If Ch. 3 showed stock-picking is a lottery, bought options are the same lottery with an expiry date.
“Fine, no stock picking — I'll buy the momentum/value factor ETF instead.”
Factor ETFs are the index-fund version of the strategies in Ch. 5, and they inherit the same fate plus fees: published factor premia decay ~58% after publication [10], most factor ETFs launched after the factor's famous decade, and none of the major U.S. factor ETFs (momentum, value, quality, low-vol) has beaten QQQ over its life. Specialized and thematic ETFs are worse — they launch at peak hype and underperform by ~6%/yr over their first five years, losing ~30% risk-adjusted [23]. An ETF wrapper changes the fees, not the arithmetic.
“I'll use stop-losses so my downside is capped.”
A stop-loss converts a temporary decline into a permanent exit — and Ch. 4.3 showed the era's best stocks fell −67% to −87% on the way to their legendary gains; any stop tight enough to 'protect' you guarantees you sell every future winner during its ordinary crashes. On the index itself, stop-and-re-enter rules are just trend-timing, which failed the Ch. 7 audits (results that depend on the trade date). The honest way to cap downside is position size decided in advance (11.4), not an automated promise to sell low.
“My newsletter/analyst has a great track record.”
The oldest result in empirical finance, from 1933: Alfred Cowles collected ~12,000 professional forecasts and asked Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast? — his abstract answer: 'It is doubtful' [27]. Sixty years later, Graham & Harvey graded 326 investment newsletters over 13 years: no evidence of any timing ability as a group, and the hot ones didn't stay hot [28]. Combine that with Ch. 6's luck math (track records arise by chance in the thousands) and the five audits (Ch. 5): a track record you're shown is marketing until it survives the audits — and in 90+ years of checking, essentially none have.
“But the test itself is flawed…”
“Is this just a U.S. / QQQ phenomenon?”
No — the lottery structure is global. Bessembinder's worldwide follow-up (64,000+ stocks across 42 countries, 1990–2018): the top 1.3% of firms produced ALL $44.7 trillion of global net wealth creation; outside the U.S., less than 1% of firms did — and 61% of non-U.S. stocks returned less than U.S. Treasury bills [31]. SPIVA runs the professional scorecard in every region — Europe, Japan, Australia, India — with the same shape of result as the U.S. The mechanisms in this study (skew, concentration, the arithmetic of active management) are properties of equity markets, not of one country or one index.
“Backtests aren't real life.”
Correct — real life is worse for the picker. These simulations already charge trading costs and use only information available at each decision date, but they can't charge you the behavioral tax: real investors panic-sell in crashes and chase after rallies, which studies of actual investor returns put at another 1–2%/yr of loss. Every gap between simulation and reality widens the index's lead.
“QQQ just got lucky — tech happened to win this era.”
Partly true — Ch. 2.2 gives this objection a full four-step answer, and the paper concedes the era-dependence openly (Ch. 9): QQQ-DCA is a concentrated bet that suffered −81% in the dot-com crash. But the anti-picking verdict does not depend on QQQ: pickers failed against SPY too, professionals fail against every benchmark (SPIVA), and the skew/concentration math (Ch. 3) holds in every equity market ever measured. Choose a broader index if you prefer — just don't hand-pick stocks against it.
“If everyone indexes, markets break and picking will work again.”
A theoretical limit that is nowhere near binding: trillions still trade actively every day, and price discovery needs remarkably little active money. If indexing ever did create big, easy mispricings, the professional funds already armed and paid to exploit them would harvest them long before a retail picker could. You don't need to volunteer to be the market's price-setter.
“Isn't QQQ itself just someone's stock picks?”
It's a rule, not a judgment: the 100 largest non-financial Nasdaq companies, weighted by size, membership refreshed mechanically. Nobody decides 'this company will win'; the weighting means whatever wins automatically becomes a bigger holding, and whatever dies falls out on its own. That mechanical winner-riding — free, tax-deferred, emotionless — is precisely the thing discretionary picking keeps failing to replicate.
“A 10-year backtest window is cherry-picked.”
The core statistics were measured across every era in 26 years of data, including both crashes: the fraction of stocks beating QQQ was computed for each year 2000–2025 (average 42%, Ch. 3), the winner-persistence numbers span 24 annual cohorts, and the strategy tests were run and re-run across eras, both halves of the sample, and dozens of start dates. The conclusion is the same in every honest slice — with one telling exception: most stocks 'beat QQQ' in 2000–2001, when QQQ itself was collapsing. Beating the index is easiest when the index is losing.
Practical questions
“Should I DCA or invest a lump sum?”
If you already have a lump sum, history favors investing it immediately about two times in three (markets rise more often than not, so waiting has a cost). DCA in this paper means the thing almost everyone actually does: investing money as it's earned, every paycheck. The point is not the cadence — biweekly vs monthly was tested and is a rounding error — it's the automation: no decisions, no timing, no picks.
“What about gold, crypto, real estate, international stocks?”
Different question — and Ch. 2.4 now treats each one fully, with the measured record. In short: those are asset-allocation choices — how much risk, of which kinds, you want to hold — and diversification genuinely smooths the ride (at some cost to return). This paper is about one specific claim: that you can select stocks to beat a stock index. Nothing here argues against owning other assets; everything here argues against paying anyone (including yourself) to pick stocks.
“Can I pick a few stocks just for fun?”
Yes — with a budget. Carve out a small fixed slice (say 5%), call it entertainment, and measure it honestly against what the same dollars would have done in QQQ. Expect it to lag (that's what the evidence says it will do), enjoy it like any hobby with a cost, and never let it grow into the plan. The danger isn't the 5% — it's the promotion of a lucky year into a strategy.
“What would it actually take to beat the index?”
The research measured this precisely: surprisingly little skill — if the errors are independent. A simulated picker with only a slight, honest edge whose mistakes were random beat QQQ in every era. What no tested signal achieved is that error-independence: every public-data signal's bold picks are the same crowded bet in different tickers, so they fail together. Genuinely independent edges come from information about individual companies that others don't have — which, for a private individual trading public information, is either unavailable or illegal to use.
Chapter 11The playbook

Evidence without instructions is trivia. Here's exactly what to do.

The complete, quantified playbook this research supports — including honest answers to "how much?", "what about picking a few stocks anyway?", and "when do I sell?".

11.1  First, the order of operations (before any investing)

  • Cash buffer first: 3–6 months of expenses. Its job is to make sure you are never forced to sell stocks in a crash.
  • Free money second: any employer retirement match, always, fully.
  • Tax-advantaged accounts before taxable (401k/IRA equivalents): the same QQQ-DCA compounds meaningfully faster untaxed.
  • Only money you won't need for 10+ years goes into equities. The index fell −81% once (Chapter 9) and −32% on a DCA account twice in 20 years. Money you'll need within ~5 years doesn't belong in it — being forced to sell into a crash is the one unrecoverable mistake.

11.2  How much goes into QQQ-DCA? All of your long-term money — if you pass the stomach test

This study's answer is simple: every dollar you are investing for 10+ years goes into the automatic QQQ purchase. Not because QQQ can't fall — but because every tested attempt to improve on it (picking, timing, rotating, waiting) ended with less money. There is no clever remainder to allocate; the schedule IS the strategy. The honest price of that answer, quantified, so you can pre-commit with open eyes:

  • A steady QQQ-DCA account fell −32% from its peak twice in 20 years (2008–09, 2022) — and kept buying. Both times the automatic contributions bought the bottom, and the account went on to new highs. The people who lost were the ones who stopped.
  • The worst case on record is worse: a lump sum at the March-2000 top fell −81% and took ~15 years to break even. A contributor through that same disaster recovered far sooner and ended far ahead — steady buying through the crash is precisely what repairs it (Chapter 2's table starts at that worst possible moment and still ends at 16× the money in).
  • So the sizing question is not a percentage — it's this stomach test: when (not if) the account shows −30%, will you keep the automation on? If yes: all long-term money, full weight. If honestly no: automate the largest amount for which the answer is yes — a plan you hold at the bottom beats a bigger plan you abandon there (the measured cost of self-inflicted timing is 1–2 points a year [15], and far worse when it happens inside a crash).
  • Money needed within ~5 years stays out entirely (11.1). Five-to-ten-year money: partial, scaled to how certain the need is.

That's the only theme pattern with a winning record: picks-and-shovels incumbents, not frontier stories.

If you're going to hunt anyway, the protocol (each rule maps to a measured failure above):

  • Keep it inside the 5–10% satellite (11.4) — sized by the Kelly logic for a negative-expectancy bet: the measured edge of picking is negative, so the "optimal" aggressive size is zero and anything you allocate is priced as entertainment/tuition. The cap converts a plan-killer into a hobby with a known cost (−3% to −6% of lifetime wealth).
  • Hunt where the required edge could actually exist (Chapter 6c): your own professional domain — the industry you genuinely know better than analysts covering 40 companies. That is the only legal channel for the uncrowded, company-specific information the math requires. If your idea comes from a feed, a ranking, or a theme ETF launch, it is by definition crowded — the measured odds of those were 5–17%.
  • Prefer the boring implementation of the trend — the SMH pattern: incumbent suppliers with profits today, not stories with promises. Wait out the hype phase: theme funds launch at peak excitement and shed ~6%/yr for five years after [23]; the survivors are still there after the crash.
  • Buy 8–10 tickets, equal-sized, and hold five-plus years. Here's the striking arithmetic: with the measured 17% chance each pick 10×'s over two decades, holding 8 tickets gives you a 77% chance of owning at least one 10-bagger — and still only a ~6% chance the basket beats QQQ. Both at once. You will very likely get a trophy and lose the race — which is exactly why everyone knows someone with a great pick and almost no one who beat the index. Decide in advance which you're playing for.
  • Expectations, measured: a 90/10 core-satellite (satellite performing at the measured picker median) ends a decade at ≈96% of pure QQQ-DCA wealth; a lucky (90th-percentile) satellite gets you to ≈99%; an unlucky one ≈94%. The satellite decides your stories; the core decides your wealth.
  • Review yearly against QQQ; feed winners' trims to the core; never feed losers. (11.4's rules apply unchanged.)

11.7  The do-not list (each one measured somewhere above)

  • No waiting in cash for crashes (Chapter 7: cost ~30% of final wealth).
  • No on/off market timing switches (Chapter 7: trade-day lottery).
  • No leverage you haven't priced at −84% (Chapter 8).
  • No selling winners to buy losers, anywhere, ever (Chapters 3–4).
  • No strategies sold on a backtest that hasn't passed the five audits (Chapter 5).
  • No acting on streaks — yours or anyone's (Chapter 6: three 10-year streaks per 10,000 coin-flippers).

How to prove this study wrong

A real study states its falsification conditions. This one is wrong if any of the following is produced: (1) a rule-based strategy, specified in advance, that beats QQQ-DCA on point-in-time delisting-inclusive data and passes all five audits (random-picker null, lead-timing, other eras, other trade days, honest costs); (2) a picker or fund, open to ordinary investors at ordinary size, with 15+ years of audited live returns above QQQ net of fees — enough to clear the skill-detection bar in Chapter 6c; or (3) a demonstration that the base-rate measurements here (6.2%, 42%, 4.6%, 0.18%) are materially wrong on equivalent data. Limitations & robustness (stated plainly)

  • Delisting convention: headline decade stats count disappeared stocks at their final traded price (acquisitions exit at deal price). Under the harshest alternative (every disappearance = −100%), the share of stocks beating QQQ moves from 6.2% to 5.9% — the conclusion is insensitive to the convention.
  • Window robustness: the core result was measured three independent ways — one full decade (2016–26: 6.2% beat), twenty-six annual cohorts (2000–25: average 42% beat over 12 months, majority-beat only when QQQ itself was crashing), and a 21-year lottery (2005–26: 4.6% beat). All agree.
  • Granularity: stock-level analysis is monthly; it cannot see intraday effects (which the day-trading literature covers, and which are worse for retail [14][16][25]).
  • Scope: U.S. common stocks only; QQQ measured as the actual ETF (fees included). Taxes are not modeled — omitting them flatters every challenger, since the challengers trade and the benchmark doesn't.
  • The era caveat is Ch. 9's: 1999–2026 is one long sample dominated by U.S. large-cap tech; the anti-picking mechanisms (skew, concentration, arithmetic of active management) are era-independent, but QQQ's specific margin over broader indexes is not.
  • Simulations are simulations: every number here is a backtest or historical measurement, not a guarantee; the behavioral evidence [14][15][16] suggests live results for active approaches would be worse, not better.

The verdict, after 26 years of honest data: no method of picking stocks or timing markets reliably ends up with more money than automatically buying QQQ on schedule. The schedule is the strategy.

Not expert selection, not "buying the winners," not value screens, not dip-buying, not machine learning. This isn't because markets are magic. It's arithmetic: a few huge winners produce nearly all stock-market wealth, the index already owns them at full size and rides them automatically, and every act of picking makes you own less of them.
